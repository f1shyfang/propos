# 02 — Australian Buyer's Agent Market Analysis

## 1. Australian Buyer's Agent Market Size

### 1.1 Total Addressable Market (TAM)

The Australian real estate sector employed over 243,000 people as of the latest REIA reporting [Source: REIA, 2026]. This figure includes all roles across sales, property management, administration, and ancillary services — not solely licensed agents.

The number of individually licensed real estate agents and agent's representatives in Australia is not published as a single national figure. Each state and territory maintains its own licensing register under separate legislation, and no federal body aggregates total licensed agent counts. As a **best estimate**, based on REIA's statement that its member institutes represent approximately 85% of all Australian real estate businesses and agents, combined with state-level licensing data, the total population of licensed real estate agents and representatives in Australia is estimated in the range of **60,000–80,000 individuals**. This estimate is approximate; a precise figure would require aggregating data from all eight state/territory fair trading registers [Source: REIA, 2026; REBAA, 2026].

Buyer's agents (BAs) represent a small fraction of the total agent population. The Real Estate Buyers Agents Association of Australia (REBAA), established in 2000, is the sole national industry body representing buyer's agents [Source: REBAA, 2026]. REBAA membership figures are not publicly disclosed in aggregate, but the association's member directory suggests a membership in the low hundreds. Not all practising buyer's agents are REBAA members.

The estimated number of actively practising, licensed buyer's agents in Australia is approximately **500–1,000 individuals**, with a substantial proportion operating as sole traders or within small agencies of 2–10 agents [Source: Estimated from REBAA membership and industry reports; no single public registry exists]. Some buyer's agents also hold selling agent licences and operate dual-practice models, blurring the boundary.

**Important caveat on market size:** This 500–1,000 estimate is derived from REBAA membership figures plus extrapolation — it is NOT a verified census. REBAA membership is voluntary, and many practising BAs are not members. Conversely, some REBAA members may be inactive or part-time. **Before committing significant resources, the team should purchase or access state-level real estate licensing databases and conduct a census of BA-licensed individuals.** Each state fair trading body (NSW Fair Trading, Consumer Affairs Victoria, etc.) maintains licence registers that typically distinguish between licence types. Aggregating this data would provide a verified TAM baseline. The 500–1,000 figure should be treated as a planning estimate with a high margin of error (±30–40%).

### 1.2 Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM)

Of the estimated 500–1,000 buyer's agents, the overwhelming majority operate as sole practitioners or within small agencies. Industry observation and REBAA's own published materials confirm that typical buyer's agency structures are:
- Sole operators (one principal licensee)
- Small agencies (2–5 agents under one licence)
- Boutique firms (5–10 agents, often multi-state)

Large-scale BA firms with more than 20 agents are rare in the Australian market. This is consistent with the service-intensive, high-touch nature of the BA role, which limits scalability per agent [Source: REBAA, 2026].

The SAM is therefore effectively the entire practising BA population: **500–1,000 individuals/agencies** across all eight states and territories, concentrated in NSW, VIC, and QLD.

### 1.3 Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM)

For a three-person hackathon team launching a SaaS platform for buyer's agents, a realistic first-year SOM target would be:

| Year | Target Accounts | Approx. Seats | Penetration (accounts of total) | Penetration (seats of total agents) |
|------|----------------|---------------|-------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Year 1 | 50–150 | 80–230 | 5–15% | 8–31% |
| Year 3 | 200–300 | 350–525 | 20–30% of accounts | 35–70% of agents |

**Note on Year 1 range:** The upper bound (150 accounts, 230 seats) represents a realistic scenario if early-adopter BAs are concentrated and the "consolidation dividend" (replacing 6-8 tools) drives rapid conversion. The lower bound is conservative. Actual Year 1 penetration depends on GTM execution and data licensing timeline.

This assumes a focused go-to-market in NSW, VIC, and QLD first, then national expansion. Early adopters in the BA space are likely to be tech-forward sole operators and small agencies currently using fragmented tools (spreadsheets, generic CRMs, manual workflows).

## 2. Buyer's Agent Adoption by State

### 2.1 Geographic Concentration

Buyer's agent activity correlates strongly with property market size and transaction values. The concentration by state, in approximate order of BA density, is:

1. **New South Wales (Sydney)** — Largest BA market. Sydney has the highest median house price nationally ($1,791,643 as of March 2026) and the highest transaction values, creating the greatest value case for BA services [Source: Domain House Price Report, March 2026].
2. **Victoria (Melbourne)** — Second-largest BA market. Melbourne median house price $1,082,728. BA usage is growing as the market recovers from recent price plateaus [Source: Domain, March 2026].
3. **Queensland (Brisbane/South East QLD)** — Fastest-growing BA market. Brisbane median house price $1,212,195 with 20.4% annual growth. Strong interstate migration is driving demand for BAs from out-of-state buyers [Source: Domain, March 2026].
4. **Western Australia (Perth)** — Perth house prices grew 24.6% YoY, the fastest nationally, driving BA demand from east-coast investors [Source: Domain, March 2026].
5. **South Australia, ACT, Tasmania, Northern Territory** — Smaller but growing BA presence.

### 2.2 Licensing Requirements by State

Each state/territory has its own licensing regime. There is no national buyer's agent licence — BAs must hold a real estate licence in each state in which they transact. Key differences [Source: REBAA, 2026]:

| State | Licence Required | Key Qualification |
|-------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **NSW** | Class 1 Real Estate Licence (principal) or Certificate of Registration (employee) | Certificate IV in Real Estate Practice |
| **VIC** | Full Real Estate Agent Licence | Certificate IV + 12 months supervised experience |
| **QLD** | Full Real Estate Agent Licence (principal) or Registration Certificate (employee) | Certificate IV + prescribed training |
| **WA** | Real Estate Agent Licence | Certificate IV + fit and proper person test |
| **SA** | Registered Land Agent (principal) or Sales Representative | Certificate IV; Diploma for principal |
| **TAS** | Full Real Estate Agent Licence | Diploma of Property Services + 2 years experience |
| **ACT** | Class 1 Real Estate Licence | Equivalent to NSW requirements |
| **NT** | Real Estate Agent Licence | Certificate IV in Real Estate Practice |

Multi-state operators must hold licences in each jurisdiction. This is a significant operational friction for BAs, particularly those serving investors or relocating buyers across state lines.

### 2.3 BA Usage Among Property Purchasers

The percentage of Australian property purchases involving a buyer's agent is not systematically tracked by any government or industry body and **cannot be verified from public data**. Industry commentary (REBAA, property media) suggests BA usage is substantially below comparable English-speaking markets such as the United States, where buyer's agent representation on purchases exceeds 80%. Australian estimates from industry commentary place the figure in the range of **5–10% of purchases** using a BA, with higher rates among investors (possibly 15–25%) and much lower among first-home buyers. This is an estimate, not a verified statistic.

The 5-year trend points to growing adoption, driven by:
- Increasing market complexity (auction culture, off-market transactions)
- More interstate and expat buyers requiring local representation
- Rising property prices making mistakes more costly
- Greater awareness of BA services through media and industry promotion

### 3.2 Buyer's Agent Fee Structures

Per REBAA, the industry standard fee for a full-service engagement (search, evaluate, negotiate, settlement) is **2–3% of the purchase price plus GST**, typically with an upfront engagement fee [Source: REBAA, 2026]. Alternative structures include:

| Fee Type | Typical Range | Usage |
|----------|--------------|-------|
| **Full service (% of purchase)** | 2.0–3.0% + GST | Most common |
| **Flat/fixed fee** | Varies by price band (e.g. $15,000–$25,000 for $1M–$1.5M range) | Growing in popularity |
| **Retainer + success fee** | Engagement fee ($2,000–$5,000) + success fee | Common for high-end |
| **Auction bidding only** | $500–$2,000 per auction | Ancillary service |
| **Single property report** | $500–$1,500 | Partial service |

## 4. Key Market Trends

### 4.1 Rising Adoption of Buyer's Agents

Multiple structural factors are driving increased BA adoption [Sources: REBAA, 2026; Domain Research, 2025–2026; CoreLogic/Cotality Insights]:

- **Competitive auction markets**: Sydney and Melbourne auction clearance rates require skilled negotiation. BAs offer bidding expertise that private buyers lack.
- **Time-poor professionals**: Dual-income households with limited weekend capacity for inspections increasingly outsource property search.
- **Property as wealth strategy**: SMSF property investment, negative gearing strategies, and intergenerational wealth transfer are increasing investment property purchases — a BA-heavy segment.
- **Interstate and expat buyers**: Post-COVID migration patterns have created demand for BAs who can represent buyers remotely (e.g. Melbourne/Sydney buyers purchasing in Brisbane or Perth).

### 4.2 Off-Market Property Demand

Access to off-market and pre-market listings is identified by REBAA as a key differentiator for BAs. Selling agents increasingly offer properties to trusted BAs before public listing. REBAA members report a growing proportion of client purchases sourced off-market, particularly in supply-constrained suburbs [Source: REBAA, 2026]. This trend benefits BAs and creates demand for CRM/tracking tools that can manage off-market pipeline properties.

### 4.3 Technology Gap

The BA industry is technologically underserved. Most sole operators and small agencies use:
- Generic CRMs not purpose-built for buyer-side workflows
- Spreadsheets for property shortlists and due diligence tracking
- Manual processes for client reporting
- Disconnected tools for communication, document management, and settlement coordination

There is no dominant, vertically integrated SaaS platform for the buyer's agent workflow in Australia. This represents a significant opportunity for a purpose-built platform.

### 4.4 Migration and Market Fragmentation

Internal migration continues to drive demand for BAs. Queensland and Western Australia are net beneficiaries of interstate migration from NSW and VIC [Source: ABS, 2025; CoreLogic/Cotality Insights, 2026]. Buyers moving interstate are disproportionately likely to engage a BA due to lack of local market knowledge. Regional markets are also experiencing relative outperformance, creating demand for BAs operating outside capital cities [Source: CoreLogic/Cotality, 2026].

## 5. Regulatory Landscape

### 5.1 Licensing and Conduct Regulation

Each state and territory regulates real estate agents (including BAs) through its own fair trading or consumer affairs body [Source: REBAA, 2026]:

| State | Regulator | Key Act |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| NSW | NSW Fair Trading | Property and Stock Agents Act 2002 |
| VIC | Consumer Affairs Victoria | Estate Agents Act 1980 |
| QLD | Office of Fair Trading | Property Occupations Act 2014 |
| WA | Consumer Protection WA | Real Estate and Business Agents Act 1978 |
| SA | Consumer and Business Services | Land Agents Act 1994 |
| TAS | Consumer, Building and Occupational Services | Property Agents and Land Transactions Act 2016 |
| ACT | Access Canberra | Agents Act 2003 |
| NT | Consumer Affairs NT | Agents Licensing Act 1979 |

BAs must hold a licence, maintain professional indemnity insurance, operate trust accounts for client funds in some states, and comply with codes of conduct. REBAA publishes its own Code of Conduct (2024 Edition) that exceeds minimum statutory requirements [Source: REBAA, 2026].

### 5.2 Consumer Protection

Key consumer protections relevant to BAs:
- **Duty of disclosure**: BAs must disclose any conflicts of interest, including any commissions or referral fees received from third parties (e.g. developers, mortgage brokers). REBAA-accredited agents must be "exclusive" — they cannot accept any payments from vendors or developers.
- **Agency agreements**: Written agreements are required before engagement, specifying services and fees. NSW Fair Trading provides standard templates.
- **Professional indemnity insurance**: Mandatory in all states for licensed agents.
- **Trust account obligations**: BAs handling client deposits or purchase funds may be required to operate audited trust accounts.

### 5.3 Privacy Act 1988 Implications

The *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth) applies to any BA operating through an entity with annual turnover exceeding $3 million, or any BA handling tax file numbers or providing services to the Commonwealth [Source: OAIC, Privacy Act 1988]. The 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) impose obligations regarding:

- **Collection of personal information** (APP 3): BAs collect extensive financial data (income, borrowing capacity, deposit sources), identification documents, and family/employment details. Collection must be reasonably necessary and by lawful means.
- **Use and disclosure** (APP 6): Client financial data exchanged with mortgage brokers, conveyancers, and selling agents must be handled in accordance with the client's consent.
- **Data security** (APP 11): BAs must take reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss, unauthorised access, modification, or disclosure.
- **Notifiable Data Breaches**: Breaches likely to result in serious harm must be reported to the OAIC and affected individuals.

The ongoing review of the Privacy Act (Privacy Act Review) may introduce additional obligations including a right of erasure and stricter consent requirements [Source: OAIC, 2025]. Any BA platform handling client financial and identity data must implement robust data protection, consent management, and breach notification capabilities.

### 5.4 Upcoming Regulatory Changes

- **Privacy Act reform**: The Australian Government's review of the Privacy Act 1988 is ongoing, with potential changes to consent requirements, individual rights, and enforcement powers. Implementation timeline uncertain [Source: OAIC].
- **AML/CTF expansion**: The real estate sector may be brought into the expanded AML/CTF regime (tranche 2), which would require BAs to conduct customer due diligence and report suspicious matters. REIA is actively engaging on this issue [Source: REIA, 2026].
- **State-level reforms**: NSW recently concluded a review of the Property and Stock Agents Act. REBAA provided strategic advice to ensure BA activities are appropriately addressed in legislative amendments. Similar reviews are underway in VIC, QLD, and WA [Source: REBAA, 2026].

### 5.5 US NAR Settlement — Potential Australian Regulatory Precedent

In March 2024, the National Association of Realtors (NAR) in the United States agreed to a **$418 million settlement** to resolve class-action lawsuits challenging the mandatory coupling of buyer agent and seller agent commissions through MLS (Multiple Listing Service) rules. Key outcomes of the settlement [Source: NAR, Burnett v. NAR et al., March 2024]:

- **Decoupling of commissions**: Seller's agents are no longer required to offer compensation to buyer's agents through the MLS. Buyer's agent compensation must now be negotiated separately and directly between the buyer and their agent.
- **Written buyer agreements**: Buyer's agents must enter into written representation agreements with buyers before showing properties, specifying the agent's compensation.
- **Effective date**: Rule changes took effect August 17, 2024.

This settlement represents the most significant structural change to US real estate agent compensation in decades. While Australian law and practice differ from the US (Australia does not have an MLS-based commission coupling system), there are potential regulatory follow-on effects:

1. **ACCC attention**: The ACCC has historically reviewed real estate industry practices. A 2024-25 ACCC inquiry into residential property transaction costs or agent conduct could examine whether BA fee structures are transparent and competitive.
2. **State-level reforms**: NSW Fair Trading, Consumer Affairs Victoria, and equivalent regulators may use the NAR settlement as a reference point when reviewing agent conduct rules, particularly around disclosure of fees and conflicts of interest.
3. **Fee disclosure standards**: Any move toward mandatory written fee agreements (beyond existing agency agreement requirements) would formalise BA pricing but could also increase compliance burden.
4. **Buyer awareness**: Increased media coverage of the NAR settlement has raised awareness among Australian property buyers about what buyer's agents charge and how — potentially affecting fee expectations and willingness to pay.

**Impact on our platform**: If Australia moves toward mandated written buyer agreements and fee disclosure, our platform's built-in buyer brief, engagement agreement templates, and fee-tracking workflow become compliance infrastructure rather than optional tools — a potential tailwind for adoption. Conversely, if regulatory changes cap or restrict BA fee structures, the BA's willingness to pay for software could be affected.
